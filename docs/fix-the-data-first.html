Fix the Data Before You Pick the Model

Author

Changez Akram

Published

November 1, 2026

I built and tested an AI assistant using five public banking regulatory documents to understand what improves answer quality. Across 26 test questions, improving document preparation and retrieval increased answer completeness from 42% to 95% on the 19 questions scored for completeness. Switching language models made a much smaller difference—just one question.

The models differed more noticeably in how they handled information they couldn’t find.

1 Why I built it

Many enterprise AI use cases involve answering questions from internal documents, policies, and procedures. Banks are increasingly evaluating these systems, often through vendor demonstrations that showcase what the technology can do.

But a successful demonstration tells us little about where a system fails, why it fails, or how reliably it answers questions. I wanted to understand what actually improves answer quality: better models, better documents, or better retrieval.

2 What I built

The assistant answers questions using five publicly available regulatory documents, citing the source document and page so readers can verify its responses. When the documents do not contain enough information to answer a question, the assistant is instructed to say so.

Document Issuer
SR 26-2, Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management (2026) Federal Reserve, FDIC, OCC
SR 11-7, Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management (2011, superseded) Federal Reserve, OCC
AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (AI 100-1) NIST
Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1) NIST
Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships: Risk Management (2023) Federal Reserve, FDIC, OCC

Each question runs through the same steps:

  1. Retrieve: Search documents using both semantic similarity and exact terms, then combine the results.
  2. Provide context: Pass the five most relevant passages, along with neighboring passages, to the language model.
  3. Generate an answer: Instruct the model to answer using only the retrieved material, cite its sources, or decline when the information is insufficient.

I developed and tested the assistant locally using Llama 3.1 8B. The publicly available version uses Claude Haiku 5.5 and is hosted online. The code and evaluation framework are available on GitHub.

3 How I tested it

I wrote 26 test questions. Twenty-one have answers in the documents: definitions, lists, exact terms, and comparisons across two documents. Five do not, such as Basel III capital requirements, the EU AI Act and CCAR stress testing.

The unanswerable questions matter as much as the others. A bank needs a system that declines to answer as reliably as it answers.

Each answer was scored the same way:

  • An answer key. For 19 questions I listed the facts a complete answer must contain, such as the five stages of the third-party life cycle. A script checks each answer for those facts by plain text match, not by asking a model.
  • The same key for every run. I wrote the key after run 3, then applied it to the saved answers from every run, including the first three.

Two open-ended questions—one about terminating third-party relationships and another comparing governance frameworks—were excluded from automated completeness scoring because they could not be reliably evaluated using a fixed list of required facts. Both remained in the 26-question test suite.

Each run changed one thing, and every run re-tested all 26 questions.

4 What moved the numbers

Changes to the documents and the search raised completeness from 42% to 95%. The model swap in run 9 moved it by one question. Refusals stayed at 100% (5 of 5) in every run.

Run Change Problem it addressed Key facts present
1 Baseline: hybrid search, Llama 3.1 8B Starting point 42%
2 Label each passage with its document name Questions about SR 26-2 retrieved other documents 63%
3 Search only the documents a question names SR 26-2’s one footnote on generative AI was buried under NIST’s generative AI pages 74%
4 Re-run of run 3 Repeatability check 74%
5 Include the passages on either side of each search hit Lists that ran across passages arrived in pieces 84%
6 Add a glossary that maps everyday words to regulatory words “Worsened” never matched NIST’s “exacerbated” 89%
7 Remove the Federal Register commentary pages 24 pages of public-comment responses crowded out the guidance 95%
8 Move search into the app so it can be hosted Hosting change 89%
9 Claude Haiku 5.5 writes the answers Model swap 95%

Every run is scored against the same final answer key. I corrected two key entries after reading answers with equivalent wording (“certain circumstances” for “urgent,” “safely and soundly” for “safe and sound”), then re-scored all nine runs, so each comparison is like for like. With 19 keyed questions, one question moves the score about 5 points, so runs 7 to 9 sit within one question of each other. The hosted system people can try is run 9’s configuration.

The largest single gain came from labeling each passage with its document name, a data change rather than a model change. Removing pages mattered too. The Federal Register version of the third-party guidance opens with background and responses to public comments. Those pages use headings such as “Due Diligence and Collaborative Arrangements,” and the model built its answer from them instead of the guidance.

5 Grounded is not correct

A common way to evaluate these systems is to have a model judge whether each answer is supported by its sources. In run 4, that judge rated 20 of 20 answers fully grounded. The answer key found 5 of 19 incomplete or unanswered.

The judge and the key were measuring different things. A groundedness check asks whether the answer stayed inside its sources. It does not ask whether the answer is complete, or whether each quote is attributed to the right document.

Two failures show the gap:

  • An invented list. Asked for the stages of the third-party life cycle, the model listed “Onboarding” and “Offboarding.” Neither appears in the guidance, which names planning, due diligence and selection, contract negotiation, ongoing monitoring, and termination. The judge rated the answer fully grounded.
  • A misattributed quote. Asked what changed between SR 11-7 and SR 26-2 on effective challenge, the model quoted SR 26-2’s language and attributed it to SR 11-7. The quote was real, so a groundedness check would pass it.

The judge was the same model that wrote the answers. Model risk managers will recognize the problem: a model reviewing its own work is not effective challenge. An answer key written by someone who knows the material is.

6 What the model changed

On completeness, the two models were close. On the same hosted setup, Llama 3.1 8B scored 89% and Claude Haiku 5.5 scored 95%: one question apart out of 19. Llama also reached 95% in run 7, with the original search setup.

Both models missed the same fact: the fifth category of NIST’s GOVERN function. When two very different models miss the same thing, the passage almost certainly never reached them. That is a search problem, and no model fixes it.

The difference was in how each model handled what its sources did not say:

  • Llama filled gaps. Earlier in the project it supplied plausible content when passages ran short, including the invented life-cycle stages. It also left claims uncited.
  • Claude named the gaps. “The excerpts name these activities but do not describe them further.” “The SR 26-2 passages provided don’t include that language.” It cited every claim, and it flagged on its own that SR 11-7 is labeled superseded.

So the finding has two parts. The knowledge and retrieval layer determines what evidence reaches the model. Model behavior also matters, particularly in how the system communicates uncertainty and handles missing evidence.

7 What this means for banks

For a bank, the investment belongs in the data and knowledge layer first. Models are improving and getting cheaper, and a bank can swap one for another. The work that made this system accurate does not transfer that way: it is specific to the bank’s own documents and vocabulary.

The most consequential improvements came from document preparation, knowledge management and retrieval engineering, not from changing the language model:

  • Document hygiene. Removing commentary, drafts and superseded versions, or labeling them clearly. Most bank document collections are full of all three.
  • Document identity. Every passage carrying its source and page, so search can find it and readers can verify it.
  • Vocabulary. A glossary mapping how employees ask to how policies are written. It is a small semantic layer, and it should be owned and reviewed like any other governed data asset.
  • Content in pictures. The guidance’s own diagram of the life cycle is an image that text extraction cannot read; the system found the stages only because an overview page also lists them in text. Bank policies are full of flowcharts and tables like it.

The regulatory picture adds urgency. SR 26-2 places generative and agentic AI outside its scope because they are “novel and rapidly evolving.” For anything outside the guidance, it says a bank’s own risk management and governance practices should decide the controls. Banks therefore have to set their own evidence standard for systems like this one. An expert-written answer key, re-run after every change, is a reasonable place to start.

8 What I would require before production

If a retrieval system came to me for approval, this is the evidence I would ask for:

  1. An answer key written by subject-matter experts, including questions the documents cannot answer.
  2. Retrieval measured separately from answers, so a search failure is never blamed on the model or hidden by it.
  3. Citation accuracy checked, meaning each claim points to the right document, not just to some source.
  4. A curated document set, with superseded versions, drafts and commentary removed or labeled.
  5. A reviewed vocabulary glossary, owned by the business that writes the policies.
  6. A plan for figures and tables, converted to text or flagged as outside the system’s reach.
  7. Refusal behavior tested, with out-of-scope questions in every evaluation run.
  8. The full test re-run after every change, not only the questions the change was meant to fix. In this project, one fix quietly broke an answer that had been correct.
  9. A model change treated as a model change, re-tested against the same key before it ships.

9 Limits of this test

This was a controlled test, not a production benchmark. Five documents and 26 questions are enough to show where the problems are, not to rank systems precisely.

  • Most test questions name their source document. Questions that don’t are likely harder.
  • The answer key checks that required facts are present, not that invented ones are absent.
  • One person wrote the key. A second reviewer would make it stronger.
  • The Claude results come from three runs, all scoring 95% on the final key.
